Pension basics Tier 2

  • You get paid 50% of your final average salary once you have 20 years.

  • Your final average salary is based off the best 3 years in a row of pay, or your last 12 months if that’s higher, overtime included, with a 20% look-back.

    What’s the 20% look-back?

    Each year in your best 3 only counts up to 120% of the average of the two years before it. Anything over that is left out. Your last 12 months work the same way: they count only up to 120% of the 12 months before them.

    Example: you made $100,000 and then $110,000. Their average is $105,000, so the next year counts only up to $126,000. If you made $140,000 that year, $14,000 of it doesn't count.

    So a big jump in one year, like a huge overtime year or a promotion, can get cut. Pay that climbs steadily over your last few years counts in full.

  • For every year you work past 20, an additional 1/60th of that pay is added on top of your pension.
  • On top of your pension, you get $12,000 a year from the VSF (Variable Supplements Fund).
What is ITHP, and the annuity?

ITHP (Increased-Take-Home-Pay) is money the City pays into your pension account for you, currently 5% of your pay, so less comes out of your check. What the City pays in after 20 years, with interest, becomes an extra pension when you retire.

If you've paid in more than the required amount, for example by keeping your contributions going after 20 years, the extra can be paid as an annuity or taken as a lump sum. Withdrawals can be taxed, and there can be a 10% penalty if you're under 50.

The calculators give a rough estimate of the ITHP piece. For the excess, type the taxable and non-taxable amounts from ESS, and they show both the lump sum and an estimated monthly annuity.

What is the 50% indicator?

It's a setting that puts extra money into your pension account, on top of what's required: an additional 50% of your required contribution, taken from each paycheck. It's voluntary.

  • The extra money is taxed right away (federal, state and city). It isn't tax-deferred like your regular contributions.
  • It earns interest at the Fund's rate, currently 8.25% a year.
  • When you retire for service or 3/4s, you can take it as a lump sum or as an extra annuity added to your pension. On an ordinary disability it has to be a lump sum.

Example: if your required contribution is 2.5% of your pay, the 50% indicator adds about 1.25% more.

It's part of the excess ESS shows you (the non-taxable part); type it in the excess boxes in either calculator. Ask the Fund or a tax professional before taking a lump sum, especially if you're under 50.

Can I borrow from my pension?

Yes. Tier 2 members can take a loan from their own contributions.

  • You need 3 years of uniformed City service.
  • You can borrow up to 75% of your contributions plus interest, and take up to 2 loans in any 12 months.
  • You pay it back through payroll deductions, at least 5% of your gross pay, and it has to be paid off by your 65th birthday. You can pay it off early any time.
  • If you die before you retire, up to $25,000 of what you owe is covered once the loan is 90 days old.

The calculators don't account for loans. Check your loan balance and any shortage with the Fund before you decide.

What is the 1/60th?

Add up everything you earn after your 20th anniversary and divide by 60. That amount is added to your yearly pension.

Example: you stay 5 years past 20 and earn $150,000 each year. That's $750,000, and 1/60th of it is $12,500 a year more pension, about $1,040 a month.

A bill in Albany, S5058A, would make it 1/40th instead. It hasn’t passed. See Bills in Albany.

When can I retire, and how much do I get?

Once you have 20 years on the job, you can retire at any age. Your pension is made of a few pieces added together:

  • 50% of your final average salary.
  • 1/60th of everything you earn after your 20th anniversary.
  • A pension from the City's ITHP money paid in after 20 years, with interest.
  • An annuity from any extra contributions left in your account, if you choose that instead of taking them as a lump sum.

Unlike Tier 3, there's no 50% cap: every year past 20 adds to your pension.

What is my final average salary?

It's the higher of two numbers, overtime, holiday pay and night differential included:

  • what you earned in the 12 months before you retire, or
  • the average of your best 3 years in a row.

There's a 20% limit. Your last 12 months count only up to 20% more than the 12 months before them. For the best 3 years, each year counts only up to 20% more than the average of the 2 years before it. Anything above that is cut. (Tier 3's limit is 10%.)

Both line up with your retirement date, not January to December. Retiring Oct. 1, for example, the years run Oct. 1 to Sept. 30. Your best 3 can be any 3 years in your career, not just your last ones.

The personal numbers page knows only each year's total, so it splits each year evenly by month. For exact numbers, add your paychecks from ESS under Find your exact final average salary.

Example: you made $100,000 and then $110,000. Their average is $105,000, so the next year counts only up to $126,000. If you made $140,000 that year, $14,000 of it doesn't count.

We're still confirming the exact rule with the Pension Fund, so treat these numbers as estimates and check your official estimate before you decide.

What if I'm hurt on the job? (3/4s)

An accident disability retirement ("three quarters") pays 75% of your final average salary, at any length of service, plus 1/60th of your earnings after 20 years, plus pensions from your ITHP account and your own contributions.

  • The 75% is free of federal tax. The 1/60th, the ITHP after 20 years and the annuity from tax-deferred contributions are federally taxed. None of it has New York State or City tax.
  • No escalation. After 5 years retired, at any age, you get the COLA (cost-of-living adjustment, which is different from full escalation): half of inflation, at least 1% and at most 3%, but only on the first $18,000 of your pension.
  • You keep health insurance no matter how many years you have.

It must be approved by the Pension Fund's Medical Board and Board of Trustees.

Do I pay taxes on my pension?

A service pension is subject to federal income tax but not New York State or New York City income tax.

For a 3/4s, see the answer above. If you move out of state, check that state's rules or ask a tax professional.

Is there an age I have to retire by?

Yes, 65. City law says uniformed members have to retire when they turn 65 (NYC Administrative Code § 15-121). It goes by your age, not your years on the job, so an earlier equated date doesn't change it.

On the personal numbers page, once you put in your date of birth, the slider stops at the most you can work before 65. Without dates it goes up to 50 years.

A bill in Albany, A9291 / S9300, would raise the age to 67. It hasn’t passed. You can see what it would mean for you on the personal numbers page, under More options.

What is an equated date?

It’s the date the Pension Fund counts your service from, and for some members it’s earlier than their appointment date. You can buy up to 3 years of military service once you have 5 years in. Uniformed service right before the FDNY, like EMS or the NYPD, can count too, and it can be much longer than 3 years. And if you were legally entitled to be hired earlier than you were, you may have been given an earlier date. Any of these gives you an earlier equated date, which moves your 20-year date, when the 1/60th and ITHP start, the banked variable and the 25-year longevity bonus.

What is the longevity bonus?

If you retire with 25 years or more, part of your rank's top pay is added to your final average salary: 5% at 25 years, plus 1% for each year after, up to 15% at 35. It became law in the 2026 state budget, signed May 27, 2026.

The law covers any member "under the plan in which they are enrolled" with the Fire Pension Fund, so it applies to Tier 2 as well as Tier 3. Its cost estimate even uses a Tier 2 firefighter as the example.

Example: a firefighter retiring at 28 years gets 8% of the top firefighter pay ($109,352), which is $8,748. That's added to their final average salary, so their pension goes up about $4,374 a year.

It doesn't apply to a 3/4s disability pension in these calculators.

Does longevity pay count?

Longevity pay is the extra money you get once you hit certain years on the job. Your pension doesn't always count all of it:

  • Retire with 20 to 24 years: only the 10-year longevity amount counts, even if your check already has more.
  • Retire with 25 years or more: the 20-year longevity amount counts.

Example: you retire at 22 years. Your check includes 20-year longevity, but your pension only counts the 10-year amount, so the difference is left out of your final average salary. Stay to 25 and all of your 20-year longevity counts.

The calculators use your full pay from your W-2, so between 20 and 25 years their numbers can run a little high. Your Fund estimate will show the exact figure.

I was out on LODI. Does that pay count?

Yes. Pay while you're out on a line-of-duty injury counts toward your pension: payroll takes your pension contribution out of it like any other pay. You don't earn overtime or night differential while you're out, though, so a long LODI can still lower that year.

But LODI pay is tax-free, so it's left out of box 5 (Medicare wages) on your W-2, and out of the NYCAPS Tax Summary the calculators import. The easiest fix: on the personal numbers page, choose Upload your W-2s in step 2, and the page reads LODI from box 14 for you. If you type in your years, put the LODI from box 14 of that year's W-2 in the LODI box next to it; the same goes if you import from NYCAPS.

The opposite goes for one-time bonuses, like the 2023 healthcare worker bonus (HWB in box 14): it's in box 5 but doesn't count toward your pension. Upload your W-2s and the page takes it out for you; if you type in your years, subtract it from that year.

This works with imported numbers too; you don't need to start over. Your Fund estimate will show the exact figure.

I was on unpaid leave (LWOP). Does that time count?

No. Time on leave without pay, like the LWOP during the 2021–22 COVID vaccine mandate, doesn't count as service. If you were out 3 months, you reach 20, 23 and 25 years 3 months later than your appointment date says.

The calculators count from your appointment date and don't know about the leave, so move the slider ahead by the time you were out.

Confirm your service dates with the Pension Fund.

Does my pension go up after I retire?

Only a little. Tier 2 has no escalation. You get the COLA: half of inflation (between 1% and 3%) on the first $18,000 of your pension, about $270 a year when inflation is 3%.

It starts at age 62 once you've been retired 5 years, or at 55 once you've been retired 10 years.

What are the VSF and the banked variable?

The VSF (Variable Supplements Fund) pays $12,000 a year to members who retire for service with 20 or more years. It's paid around December 15 for UFA members and around January 31 for UFOA members.

The banked variable: for each full month you work past 20 years, $1,000 (one month of the $12,000 VSF) is banked. You get it as a lump sum when you retire. For example, 5 extra years means $60,000, and 2 extra months means $2,000.

Until you turn 62, any COLA you get is taken out of your VSF.

Does Social Security lower my pension?

No. The Social Security offset at 62 is a Tier 3 rule. Your Tier 2 pension isn't reduced when you start Social Security.

What is the “death gamble”?

It's what your family gets if you die while still on the job with 20 or more years, from something that isn't line of duty. The Fund figures your pension as if you had retired the day before, and your beneficiary gets its reserve: the lump sum it would take to pay that pension for the rest of your life. They can take it as a lump sum or as an annuity.

  • Under 20 years: it's the ordinary death benefit instead: 3 times your last 12 months' pay (rounded up to the next $1,000), plus your contributions back with interest.
  • After you retire: the coverage lasts until your first full pension check. After that, what your family gets depends on the option you picked when you retired. With the maximum pension (no option), payments stop when you die.
  • Line of duty: a separate benefit: a pension for your spouse of 50% of your salary, plus a state benefit that brings the total, with Social Security, up to your last year's pay.
  • Life insurance: $8,500, tax-free, from the Fire Department Life Insurance Fund.

That's the "gamble": past 20, staying on the job keeps this protection for your family, and retiring on the maximum pension gives it up. The Fund can tell you what your reserve is worth and what each option would cost you.

Do I keep my health insurance?

These rules change with new laws, court rulings and union contracts. Always check your coverage directly with your health plan, your union, or the Fire Pension Fund before you make decisions.

  • Who qualifies: you need at least 10 years of credited service in a City pension system (5 years if you were a City employee on or before December 27, 2001). Retiring on an FDNY service pension meets this.
  • Only while collecting: health benefits come with collecting a pension, and they're only available while you're actually collecting one. Accident disability retirees get health insurance regardless of years of service.
  • Before 65: you stay on the same plans as active members. As of January 2026, that's the NYCE PPO.
  • At 65: you go on traditional Medicare plus a City plan such as Senior Care or HIP VIP. The City dropped its plan to move retirees to Medicare Advantage in 2025.
  • Dental, optical and prescriptions in retirement come through your union's welfare fund. Check with them for details.

For details, call Employee Health Benefits at the NYC Office of Labor Relations: 212-306-7390.

Does my other City service count? (1/80th)

Yes, if you had a non-uniformed City job before the FDNY (NYCERS or Board of Ed time) and it's credited with the Fire Pension Fund, by transfer or buyback. Uniformed service that didn't come right before the FDNY counts the same way.

For each year of that service, your pension adds 1/80th of your average pay over your last 5 years. It doesn't count toward your 20 years, and it isn't paid on a 3/4s.

Example: 3 years of other City service and $150,000 average pay over your last 5 years. 3 × $150,000 ÷ 80 is $5,625 a year more pension, about $470 a month.

Add it in step 1 of Your personal numbers. Ask the Fund what a buyback would cost.

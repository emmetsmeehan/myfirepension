Pension basics Tier 3

  • You get paid 50% of your final average salary once you have 20 years.

  • Your final average salary is based off the best 3 years in a row of pay, overtime included, with a 10% look-back.

    What’s the 10% look-back?

    Each year in your best 3 only counts up to 110% of the average of the two years before it. Anything over that is left out.

    Example: you made $100,000 and then $110,000. Their average is $105,000, so the next year counts only up to $115,500. If you made $130,000 that year, $14,500 of it doesn't count.

    So a big jump in one year, like a huge overtime year or a promotion, can get cut. Pay that climbs steadily over your last few years counts in full.

  • Once you complete 23 years, you get full escalation (a cost-of-living raise), so your whole pension keeps up with inflation.
  • On top of your pension, you get $12,000 a year from the VSF (Variable Supplements Fund).
When can I retire, and what if I leave before 20 years?

You can leave the job whenever you want. What you get depends on how many years you have:

  • Under 5 years: no pension. You get back the money you paid in (your contributions).
  • 5 to 10 years: you’re vested, so you pick one: take back the money you paid in, or leave it in and collect a vested pension starting on what would have been your 20-year date. You can’t do both.
  • 10 to 20 years: you can’t take your contributions back. You get a vested pension starting on what would have been your 20-year date.

A vested pension is 2.1% of your final average salary for each year you worked. Leave with 15 years and it’s 31.5%. It starts when you would have hit 20 years, or at age 55 with a small cut if that comes first. There’s no VSF, and it drops at 62 by half your Social Security, like a regular pension.

Once you have 20 years on the job, you can retire and collect 50% of your final average salary for the rest of your life.

The 50% doesn't go up if you stay longer. (A bill in Albany, S5058A, would add 1/40th of your final average salary for each year past 20. It hasn't passed. See Bills in Albany.) Staying can still raise your pension in three ways: your final average salary is usually higher, your pension can grow with inflation (escalation), and you bank extra VSF payments.

Tier 3 members must retire by age 62.

What is my final average salary?

It's the average of your best 3 years in a row, overtime included. They can be any 3 years in your career, not just your last ones, and each year runs from your retirement month and day, not January to December. Retire April 6, and each year runs April 6 to April 5.

The personal numbers page uses whole calendar years until you add your paychecks from ESS under Find your exact best 3. Then it counts each year the Fund's way.

There's one limit: a year only counts up to 10% more than the average of the 2 years before it. Anything above that is cut.

Example: you made $100,000 and then $110,000. Their average is $105,000, so the next year counts only up to $115,500. If you made $130,000 that year, $14,500 of it doesn't count.

What if I'm hurt on the job? (3/4s)

An accident disability retirement ("three quarters") pays 75% of your final average salary, at any length of service, and it's generally not taxed.

  • No escalation. After 5 years retired, at any age, you get the COLA (cost-of-living adjustment, which is different from full escalation): half of inflation, at least 1% and at most 3%, but only on the first $18,000 of your pension.
  • No Social Security offset at 62. Your pension doesn't drop at 62.
  • You keep health insurance no matter how many years you have.

It must be approved by the Pension Fund's Medical Board and Board of Trustees. You don't need to qualify for Social Security Disability to get a line-of-duty disability pension (3/4s).

Do I pay taxes on my pension?

A service pension is subject to federal income tax but not New York State or New York City income tax.

A line-of-duty disability pension (3/4s) is generally not taxed. If you move out of state, check that state's rules or ask a tax professional.

What is an equated date?

It’s the date the Pension Fund counts your service from, and for some members it’s earlier than their appointment date. You can buy up to 3 years of military service once you have 5 years in. Uniformed service right before the FDNY, like EMS or the NYPD, can count too, and it can be much longer than 3 years. And if you were legally entitled to be hired earlier than you were, you may have been given an earlier date. Any of these gives you an earlier equated date, which moves your 20-year date, the 23-year date for full escalation, the banked variable and the 25-year longevity bonus.

If you were out on leave without pay (LWOP), your equated date may be later than your appointment date.

What is my “minimum required,” and what if I'm in deficit?

For your first 25 years, you pay into the pension: a basic 3% of your pay, plus about 2% more. After 25 years, those payments stop, and you keep your full paycheck. Your money in the account earns 5% interest.

The extra 2% pays for the enhanced disability plan: a disability pension (3/4s) if you're hurt on the job, and a better pension if you're disabled off duty. Everyone appointed on or after June 15, 2016 pays it. Tier 3 members appointed before that had a one-time choice to join. The Fund reviews the rate every three years, and by law it can't go above 3% (RSSL §517(h)).

Your minimum required is what your account should hold for the service you have so far. If you're below it, you're in deficit.

You can't retire with a deficit. Pay it off before you go, either in one lump sum or through payroll deductions. You can check your balance on the Fund's member portal.

Can I borrow from my pension?

No. Tier 3 has no pension loans.

If you need to borrow against retirement savings, the City's 457 and 401(k) plans offer loans. See Other accounts.

Does longevity pay count?

Longevity pay is the extra money you get once you hit certain years on the job. Your pension doesn't always count all of it:

  • Retire with 20 to 24 years: only the 10-year longevity amount counts, even if your check already has more.
  • Retire with 25 years or more: the 20-year longevity amount counts.

Example: you retire at 22 years. Your check includes 20-year longevity, but your pension only counts the 10-year amount, so the difference is left out of your final average salary. Stay to 25 and all of your 20-year longevity counts.

The calculators use your full pay from your W-2, so between 20 and 25 years their numbers can run a little high. Your Fund estimate will show the exact figure.

I was out on LODI. Does that pay count?

Yes. Pay while you're out on a line-of-duty injury counts toward your pension: payroll takes your pension contribution out of it like any other pay. You don't earn overtime or night differential while you're out, though, so a long LODI can still lower that year.

But LODI pay is tax-free, so it's left out of box 5 (Medicare wages) on your W-2, and out of the NYCAPS Tax Summary the calculators import. The easiest fix: on the personal numbers page, choose Upload your W-2s in step 2, and the page reads LODI from box 14 for you. If you type in your years, put the LODI from box 14 of that year's W-2 in the LODI box next to it; the same goes if you import from NYCAPS.

The opposite goes for one-time bonuses, like the 2023 healthcare worker bonus (HWB in box 14): it's in box 5 but doesn't count toward your pension. Upload your W-2s and the page takes it out for you; if you type in your years, subtract it from that year.

This works with imported numbers too; you don't need to start over. Your Fund estimate will show the exact figure.

I was on unpaid leave (LWOP). Does that time count?

No. Time on leave without pay, like the LWOP during the 2021–22 COVID vaccine mandate, doesn't count as service. If you were out 3 months, you reach 20, 23 and 25 years 3 months later than your appointment date says.

The calculators count from your appointment date unless you enter your equated date in Your personal numbers.

It won't drag down your salary average, though. If unpaid leave falls in the years your pension is figured on, up to 12 months of it is left out and the same stretch just before those years is used instead. The calculators don't make this swap.

Confirm your service dates with the Pension Fund.

Does my pension go up after I retire?

It depends on how many years you have when you retire:

  • 23 years or more: full escalation. Your whole pension goes up with inflation every year, up to 3% a year. The NYC Office of the Actuary sets the rate each April, based on inflation through the previous December. If inflation runs higher than 3% in a year, the extra is saved and added in a later year when inflation is lower (state law, RSSL §510). The calculators assume the same inflation every year, so they don't show this.
  • Between 20 and 23 years: partial escalation. You get 1/36 of full escalation for each month past 20 years.
  • 20 years: no escalation. You only get a small COLA: half of inflation (between 1% and 3%) on the first $18,000 of your pension, about $270 a year when inflation is 3%. It starts at age 62 once you've been retired 5 years, or at 55 once you've been retired 10 years.

This changed in the 2026 state budget, signed May 27, 2026. Before that, full escalation took 25 years. Another bill (S9203) would move it to 20 years; it hasn't passed. See Bills in Albany.

Why do 23 and 25 years matter?

Completing 23 years gets you full escalation. Your pension grows every year and each raise builds on the last.

Example: a $90,000 pension that grows 3% a year is about $162,500 after 20 years. Without escalation, the same pension stays close to $90,000.

25 years starts the longevity bonus, which raises the salary your pension is figured on.

What is the longevity bonus?

If you retire with 25 years or more, part of your rank's top pay is added to your final average salary: 5% at 25 years, plus 1% for each year after, up to 15% at 35. It became law in the 2026 state budget, signed May 27, 2026, and it applies to any uniformed rank.

Example: a firefighter retiring at 28 years gets 8% of the top firefighter pay ($109,352), which is $8,748. That's added to their final average salary, so their pension goes up about $4,374 a year.

It doesn't apply to a disability pension (3/4s) in these calculators.

What are the VSF and the banked variable?

The VSF (Variable Supplements Fund) pays $12,000 a year to members who retire for service with 20 or more years. It's paid around December 15 for UFA members and around January 31 for UFOA members.

The banked variable: for each full month you work past 20 years, $1,000 (one month of the $12,000 VSF) is banked. You get it as a lump sum when you retire. For example, 5 extra years means $60,000, and 2 extra months means $2,000.

Until you turn 62, any COLA you get is taken out of your VSF.

What is the Social Security offset?

At 62, your pension goes down by half of a Social Security figure. That's the Social Security offset. It's a Tier 3 rule.

It isn't half of the Social Security check you'll actually get. The Fund works out its own figure (state law, RSSL § 511):

  • Only your pay from a public employer counts. Every other year counts as zero, so private jobs don't add to it.
  • It's set the day you retire. Later Social Security raises don't change it.
  • A second way of figuring it, from your highest pay in your last 5 years, is used if it comes out lower.
  • It's cut 20% because the offset starts before 65.

For most members that works out to roughly $700 to $900 a month off at 62. The personal numbers page estimates yours from the earnings you enter.

Escalation keeps going after 62, figured on the lower amount. Social Security itself is paid separately, by the federal government.

Albany is fighting it. S6289A (Gounardes) would end the offset for FDNY members. It's in the Senate Civil Service and Pensions Committee and hasn't passed either house. Like every 2025–26 bill, it dies Dec. 31, 2026 unless it passes.

Does Tier 3 have the “death gamble”?

No. In Tier 2, if you die on the job with 20 or more years (not line of duty), your family gets the reserve for your pension: the lump sum it would take to pay it for the rest of your life. That's the "death gamble." The Fund's Tier 3 plan description has no such benefit.

In Tier 3, if you die before you retire (not line of duty), your beneficiary gets the ordinary death benefit at any length of service: 3 times your last 12 months' pay (rounded up to the next $1,000), plus your contributions back with interest. It doesn't change at 20 years, so staying on the job longer doesn't add protection for your family the way it does in Tier 2.

  • After you retire: what your family gets depends on the option you pick when you retire. With the maximum pension (no option), payments stop when you die. Options lower your pension to leave something to a beneficiary.
  • Line of duty: a separate benefit: a pension for your spouse of 50% of your final average salary, plus a state benefit that brings the total, with Social Security, up to your last year's pay.
  • Life insurance: $8,500, tax-free, from the Fire Department Life Insurance Fund while you're active; $7,500 after you retire.

This is from the Fund's Tier 3 plan description, revised December 2019. A later law could change it, so check with the Fund.

Do I keep my health insurance?

These rules change with new laws, court rulings and union contracts. Always check your coverage directly with your health plan, your union, or the Fire Pension Fund before you make decisions.

  • Who qualifies: you need at least 10 years of credited service in a City pension system (5 years if you were a City employee on or before December 27, 2001). Retiring on an FDNY service pension meets this.
  • Only while collecting: health benefits come with collecting a pension, and they're only available while you're actually collecting one. Accident disability retirees get health insurance regardless of years of service.
  • Before 65: you stay on the same plans as active members. As of January 2026, that's the NYCE PPO.
  • At 65: you go on traditional Medicare plus a City plan such as Senior Care or HIP VIP. The City dropped its plan to move retirees to Medicare Advantage in 2025.
  • Dental, optical and prescriptions in retirement come through your union's welfare fund. Check with them for details.

For details, call Employee Health Benefits at the NYC Office of Labor Relations: 212-306-7390.

Bills in Albany affecting Tier 3

Status last checked Nov. 12, 2026.

Current session: 2025 and 2026. Any bill not signed into law by Dec. 31, 2026 dies and has to start over in 2027.

How does a bill actually become law?
  1. A bill is introduced. A senator and an Assembly member each put in their own version, which is why most bills have an S number and an A number.
  2. It goes to committee. Pension bills go to the Senate Civil Service and Pensions Committee and the Assembly Governmental Employees Committee, and each one needs a cost estimate from the City’s actuary. Most bills never get past this step.
  3. The committee votes. If it passes, it usually moves on to Finance in the Senate or Ways and Means in the Assembly, then to Rules. Leadership in each house decides which bills get a floor vote.
  4. The Senate and the Assembly both pass it, with the same wording. If one side changes the bill, the other side has to pass the changed version. That’s what the A or B at the end of a bill number means.
  5. The governor signs it or vetoes it. The legislature decides when to send a passed bill to the governor, so a bill passed in June may not reach her desk until the fall. Once it does, she has 10 days to act, or 30 days if the legislature has gone home for the year.
  6. It becomes law, with a chapter number like “Chapter 692 of 2025,” the law that let Tier 3 retire at 20 years.

Some pension changes skip these steps and go through the state budget in the spring instead, like the 23-year rule for full escalation in May 2026.
